# Story 1: The Forum

A destress fiction, built on the persona in [[persona]]. Not a real record —
keep it fictional on purpose.

**In progress.** Numbered sections, not chapters — a developer wrote this
one. Written and edited one section at a time; a section gets its number
when it's actually locked in.

## Section 1

Angel almost didn't reply to the thread. It was already fifteen comments deep,
half of them useless "just use X" drive-bys, but one reply stopped her
scrolling — some user with a plain, forgettable handle, arguing that the
popular answer everyone was upvoting was actually a bad idea dressed up as a
good one.

*"This 'works' the same way duct tape works,"* he'd written. *"It holds
until it doesn't, and then it takes the bumper with it."*

She typed a reply before she'd finished deciding to. Something sharp,
something that pushed back — she happened to like the duct tape, thank you
very much, and she explained exactly why with the confidence of someone
who'd shipped the pattern to production a hundred times.

Callen answered in eleven minutes. No hedging, no "just my opinion but." He took
her argument apart piece by piece, and somehow it didn't feel like an
attack — it felt like he'd actually read every word she wrote, closely
enough to know exactly where it was weakest.

*"I don't think you're wrong that it works,"* he wrote. *"I think you're
describing a floor and calling it a ceiling."*

She laughed out loud at her desk, alone, which was somehow worse than if
someone had seen her.

The thread went back and forth another six replies before the moderators
locked it for length. By then it wasn't really about the code anymore — it
hadn't been for a while. It was two people who'd finally found someone who
argued back instead of folding, and neither of them wanted to be the one to
let it end.

That night, a DM: *"For what it's worth — best argument I've had on that
forum in two years. Most people just agree with me because arguing is
exhausting. You didn't."*

Angel stared at the message longer than she'd admit to anyone. Then she typed
back: *"Give it time. I'm still right, I just haven't finished proving it
yet."*

## Section 2

Angel started tagging him on threads before she'd even finished forming her
own opinion, just to see what he'd say. She was the one saving her best
arguments for him specifically, half-annoyed at herself for caring that
much what anyone thought of her logic.

They fought about caching strategies for a week. He was insufferably smug
when he turned out to be right, and she made him admit it took him three
tries to explain why before it actually made sense — which he did,
grudgingly, followed by *"noted for next time you're wrong."*

They fought about naming conventions, which nearly ended in a genuine
grudge, until she sent a meme so exactly aimed at his specific pedantry that
he broke and sent back a single laughing emoji — the closest thing to
surrender he was capable of.

They fought about whether elegant code that nobody could maintain was
actually good code. That one went four days and ended in a draw neither of
them would admit to.

Somewhere in there, the DMs stopped being purely reactive. Callen would send
her a random article on Saturday with no context except *"this is either
genius or insane, tell me which."* Angel would go digging for an excuse — a
genuine bug in his repo, a question that barely needed asking — just to
watch him solve it in nine minutes flat and gloat about it.

Angel told herself that it was just good conversation and a great work
friend to have.

## Section 3

What Callen didn't realize — couldn't have, from a handle and a string of
arguments he was winning maybe half the time — was that Angel was single
for a reason, and the reason wasn't a shortage of options.

She didn't actually like arguing. Whatever the thread suggested about her,
the truth was quieter than that: conflict wore her down in a way she'd
stopped trying to explain to people who assumed the opposite. She had a
system for it, worn smooth from years of use — drop the position, wait,
see what comes back, stay only if the pushback actually engaged with what
she'd said. Otherwise she stepped back. Almost every time, she stepped
back. Not laziness. Self-preservation. Some people were built for
friction. She wasn't one of them, whatever a thread full of her best
comebacks might suggest.

It wasn't only her ex-husband who'd taught her that, though he'd done his
part — treating every disagreement like something to be won instead of
worked through, same as more than one close friend she'd since let go of.
The real shape of it went back further than any one marriage. Her father
had left before she was old enough to remember him. Her mother had hurt
her in ways parts of her own family chose not to believe, even when she
said so plainly — which taught her something worse than the hurt itself:
that telling the truth doesn't guarantee anyone will believe it, or you.
By adulthood she'd learned to just stop bothering.

Compromise had never been her problem. She was fine with compromise,
always had been. What actually happened — in the marriage, and in most of
her life before it — was never really compromise. It was her breaking,
quietly, over and over, until she believed whatever the people around her
needed her to believe, just to make the disagreement stop.

It took a lunch with her best friend, Deena, somewhere in the middle of the
divorce, to actually crack that open. Deena looked at Angel's drink and
asked how her coffee was.

*"This is Pepsi,"* Angel said, laughing.

*"No, it's coffee,"* Deena said, entirely straight-faced.

*"Look at the ice."*

*"Yeah. Iced coffee."*

Angel laughed again, waiting for the joke to land somewhere — and it
didn't. Deena just kept insisting, calm and immovable, until Angel's eyes
went hot and her voice cracked on *"Why are you doing this to me? You're my
friend. You know exactly what I'm going through right now."*

*"Because you need to learn something,"* Deena said. *"You need to learn to
stop believing everything people tell you just because they say it with
enough confidence. You're not a bad person. You weren't a bad wife. He
didn't leave because you did something wrong."*

That was the moment something finally came loose — and it wasn't small.
Angel didn't just stop believing her ex-husband's version of her. She
stopped believing her mother's, and the family's who'd rather not know
what really happened at home, and every smaller voice in between who'd
found it easier to tell her what to think than to actually ask her.
Something that had been running on pure survival for most of her adult
life started, slowly, to run on something else instead. She stopped
enduring things and started fighting for them.

And somewhere past that, she learned the second half of the lesson — the
one that actually mattered on a random Tuesday, arguing with a stranger on
a forum: it wasn't only that she didn't have to believe it was coffee. It
was that she had to get smart about who she bothered correcting at all.
Most people, handed the same glass of Pepsi, would keep calling it coffee
forever, no matter what she said, because being right about her was never
the point for them — being agreed with was. Arguing with people like that
cost her something and changed nothing. So she'd learned not to. It wasn't
peace, exactly. It was math.

Since the divorce there'd been a few guys, the ordinary kind, and every one
of them had eventually started to annoy her in the exact same quiet way.
Not because she thought she was smarter than them — she didn't, not
really, that was never the problem. It was that none of them seemed
interested in actually hearing her out. They waited for their turn to
talk. They nodded in the wrong places. They agreed too fast, which was its
own kind of not listening.

Callen argued like someone who'd actually read the whole thing first.

That, she suspected — though she wasn't ready to say it, not even to
herself, not in so many words — was exactly why she hadn't walked away
yet.

## Section 4

Neither of them noticed exactly when it happened. One day there just wasn't
a day without a message.

Angel started it more often than not, which surprised her every single time —
unprompted, deciding he was who she wanted to say it to before she'd even
finished the thought. A half-formed idea about a problem she was stuck on.
A screenshot with just *"thoughts?"* and nothing else, like his opinion had
quietly become part of her actual workflow now.

Angel would catch herself checking before her morning Pepsi. Not out of anxiety, just —
anticipation. A good kind of waiting, the kind where you already know the
answer's coming, you're just impatient for it.

One night she went quiet for a few hours, buried in a deadline, and Callen
noticed. *"You good? You went dark mid-argument, that's not like you."* Not
clingy, not dramatic — just paying attention. She hadn't realized how much
she'd wanted someone to notice the pattern of her, not just the content of
her, until someone actually did.

She started saving things throughout the day just to tell him later — not
because it was useful to him, just because she wanted to hear what he'd say
about it. He did the same, she realized, once she caught herself doing it
back.

It stopped being "he replies fast" and started being "he replies like it
matters." Every single time.

Angel didn't say any of this out loud, not even to herself in so many words.
But somewhere in the daily-ness of it, the rule they'd never said out loud —
*this stays what it is* — quietly stopped being true, and neither of them
moved to fix it.

## Section 5

Angel almost didn't send it. Stared at it for a solid minute — such a small,
throwaway-sounding thing, and yet it felt like crossing a line neither of
them had officially drawn but both of them knew was there.

*"Random question — do you do anything else for fun besides code?"*

She hit send before she could talk herself out of it, then immediately
regretted it, then immediately stopped regretting it when the reply came
back in under two minutes.

*"Depends what you mean by fun. If you mean 'things that don't involve a
keyboard' — yeah, actually. I get out a lot. Rivers, trails, anywhere with
dirt and no signal. There's something about a problem you can't argue your
way out of that I find weirdly relaxing, considering my day job is arguing
my way out of problems."*

She smiled at her screen like an idiot.

*"That tracks, honestly,"* she typed back. *"You debug code like you're
daring it to fight you. Makes sense you'd want a hobby that actually
could."*

*"Ha. Fair. Your turn — what does the undefeated forum champion do when
she's not making grown developers reconsider their life choices?"*

Nobody had asked her that in longer than she wanted to admit. Not like that
— genuinely curious, waiting for the real answer instead of already halfway
checked out.

She typed for a while, deleted it, typed again. Landed on something honest
instead of impressive.

*"Disney, unapologetically — I'm at the parks more weekends than not, and
yes, I will absolutely lose an evening to a Disney video game if I'm not up
for driving over. Outside of that, I'm an action-movie person. Jason Bourne,
Taken, Fast and Furious — anything with a plot that moves and a guy who's
disturbingly competent at one specific thing."*

*"So — chaos and competence. Consistent theme, at least."*

*"I contain multitudes."*

*"Noted,"* Callen wrote back. *"I'll try not to bore you, then."*

*"You haven't yet."*

There was a pause — a real one, longer than his usual instant-reply rhythm —
and then: *"Good. I'd like to keep it that way."*

Neither of them mentioned code again for the rest of the night.

## Section 6

Over the next few weeks, they kept finding reasons to know more about each
other than code ever required.

Marvel came up sideways — an offhand comparison, something about a plot
twist being "such an Infinity War move" — and turned into two hours where
neither of them touched code. Callen turned out to be a first-phase
loyalist, deep cuts and all; Angel fought hard for the newer stuff having
done more interesting things with the characters. It ended in a draw
neither of them would admit to, and became a running bit after that — one
more shared language, casually referenced for weeks.

Other real things came out too, spread across different nights — where
they'd each grown up, what their twenties had actually looked like versus
what they told people at parties. An offhand joke about being "too old for
this nonsense" led to an actual admission: late forties for him, early
fifties for her, and neither of them so much as blinked at the gap.

*"Good,"* he wrote. *"I don't have patience left for anyone who hasn't
lived a little first."*

*"Same. I did my time being someone's wife. I like my life now. Nobody to
answer to, no one rearranging my evenings for me."*

*"Divorced?"*

*"Divorced. You?"*

*"Same story, different chapter. Turns out I'm significantly better company
once nobody's required to put up with me."*

*"I find that very believable."*

*"Rude. Accurate. But rude."*

None of it landed all at once. It built the slow way real things actually
build — a fact here, a joke there — until a few weeks in, they knew more
about each other's actual lives than either had really planned on. Not a
shift toward anything. Just two people who argued about fictional
multiverses turning, gradually, into two people who actually knew each
other.

## Section 7

It came up naturally. Angel mentioned a name — someone she'd worked a
project with years back — and Callen went quiet for a second too long.

*"Wait. You know Mark?"*

*"...Yeah? Why?"*

*"He's one of maybe six people in this industry I actually trust. Small
world just got smaller."*

It turned out there were more overlaps than either of them expected — a
shared conference circuit, a couple of the same Slack communities, people
they'd both argued with or learned from over the years. Not close friends
of theirs, exactly, just enough shared ground to make the anonymity feel a
little thinner than before.

*"Feels weird knowing we've probably been in the same room before and just
never crossed paths,"* Angel wrote.

*"We probably have,"* Callen wrote back.

No maybe in it anywhere. She noticed that, a little — the certainty of it,
like it wasn't really a guess — and then let it go, the way you let go of
most small things that don't quite add up when there's no reason yet to
add them up.

That thread pulled a longer conversation out of both of them — comparing
notes on the actual shape of their careers, not just who they'd both
bumped into. Callen had been doing the conference circuit for years,
opinions and reputation built the slow, visible way. Angel admitted, a
little sheepishly, that she'd done the opposite for most of her career —
good at the work, careful never to be the one standing at the front of the
room talking about it. She'd only started actually going to conferences
herself in the last year or so, still getting used to being someone people
might recognize instead of just someone doing the work quietly in the
background.

*"That surprises me,"* he wrote. *"You argue like someone who's used to
being listened to."*

*"On a screen, sure,"* she wrote back. *"In a room full of people, that's
newer. I spent a long time being good at my job and terrible at being seen
doing it."*

*"What changed?"*

She thought about it longer than the question probably warranted.
*"Honestly? Arguing with you. Somewhere in the last few months I stopped
rehearsing what I was going to say before I said it. Turns out that's most
of what was actually stopping me."*

*"Seriously,"* she wrote, before she could talk herself out of it.
*"Thank you. I don't know if you realize how much you're actually a part
of that."*

There was a pause, longer than his usual rhythm. *"Noted. And you're
welcome — though for the record, I didn't do anything. You did the hard
work."*

It was a bug that did it — something neither of them could untangle over
text fast enough, and he finally just said *"screw it, can I call you,"*
and she said yes before she'd thought about what that meant.

They ended up talking for almost an hour — the bug solved somewhere in the
first twenty minutes and completely beside the point after that. Work
bled into everything else without either of them noticing the line: a
rant about a client's requirements turning into what he actually wanted to
be doing with his career, her venting about a decision at work turning
into something about Madi, and the parks, and nothing in particular,
except that neither of them wanted to be the one to end the call first.

By the time she hung up, it wasn't the bug she was thinking about, or even
the hour they'd just spent. It was how normal it had felt — like a
conversation they'd already been having for months, just finally out
loud.

## Section 8

The conference came up naturally, once the shared circles made it obvious
neither of them could avoid it even if they'd wanted to. *"Wait — are you
going to the conference this year?"* Callen wrote.

*"Yeah, flying out tomorrow. You?"* Angel wrote back.

*"Same."*

Thirty-some thousand people, a venue the size of a small city. Neither of
them suggested meeting up.

The first day was a blur of familiar faces — years of conference
friendships and old coworkers, several of them people they apparently had
in common without ever realizing it. She hugged her way through the
cluster near the coffee station without a second thought, the easy kind of
hello she'd given a hundred times before, to people she genuinely liked but
didn't know all that deeply.

Then she heard it before she saw him — that voice, unmistakable, the one
that had kept her up two hours past when she should've hung up. She turned,
arm still half-raised out of pure hug-everyone momentum, and the
recognition hit twice — the voice first, then the face underneath it,
solid and real in a way she never imagined.

She froze. Course-corrected badly. Stuck her hand out instead, all
business.

*"Hi. Nice to finally meet you in person."*

He just shook it and smiled politely, like how he had done with others.

She left the room not long after — needed air, needed a second to figure
out why her hands wouldn't stop being weird. When she came back through the
hall, he wasn't at the cluster anymore. She found him a few minutes later,
alone, walking down a deserted stretch of hallway away from the noise.

*"Hey — you good?"* she asked, catching up.

*"Yeah, fine,"* he said, pleasant and even, the exact tone he'd use with
anyone else at the conference. *"Looked like you had a full morning. Lot
of people to catch up with."*

Professional. Easy. Nothing like the voice from the call, nothing like an
hour on the phone that neither of them had wanted to end. He was talking
to her like she was a name badge, and doing it smoothly enough that she
couldn't have called it out without sounding unhinged.

It landed harder than he probably meant it to, and worse, she didn't have a
fast enough answer for it, because the truth was so much more complicated
than he could possibly know standing there. Her eyes went hot before she
could stop it.

Angel didn't find developers good-looking, nor did she think of them that
way — this industry had just never been a place she saw men like that.
Every so often over the years she'd notice one anyway, some flicker of
something, and she'd just as easily brush it off or find a reason to keep
her distance — filing him away as a work-husband instead, the guy she
cried to about the latest jerk who didn't deserve her, never the guy
himself. She dated jocks. Race-car types. Guys who were absolutely nothing like the
men she spent her actual working hours with.

Now here was her exception, Callen, standing in front of her, and she had
no hug for him at all, because a hug was for someone safe, and nothing
about this felt safe anymore.

*"It's not that,"* she finally said, voice thinner than she wanted it to
be. *"It's the opposite of that. I don't know how to explain it right now.
Just — it's not what you think."*

He didn't push, which somehow made it worse — he just stood there in the
empty hallway, arms crossed, waiting her out with that same patience he
used on a bug he knew he'd eventually win against.

## Section 9

They just looked at each other for a minute, neither of them saying
anything. Angel took a breath, deeper than she meant to, and closed the
last of the distance between them without quite deciding to.

Callen opened his arms, and then they were hugging — and it lasted far
longer than any hug she'd given anyone else that day, warmer too, in a way
that had nothing to do with how long it went on and everything to do with
how it actually felt.

Neither of them said anything for a second after that. It didn't feel like
it needed filling.

*"We should probably get back to the conference,"* Callen said finally.

*"Yeah,"* Angel agreed. *"Probably."*

Neither of them pushed it further than that. They talked a little while
longer in the hallway — nothing that mattered, everything that did — and
then she went her way and he went his.

It occurred to her, walking back toward the noise sometime after midnight,
that she had no idea whether there was someone waiting on the other end of
his phone the way there sometimes was for guys like this — some sensible,
serious girlfriend she'd never once thought to ask about, because until
tonight none of it had needed asking. She almost asked. She didn't. Some
questions felt too large to drop into a conversation this new, and this,
whatever it actually was, felt too fragile to risk breaking over the wrong
question at the wrong moment.

She barely slept that night. Every time she closed her eyes she was back
in that hallway, replaying the two seconds before the hug like she could
find some hidden clue in it if she just watched it enough times. She lay
there feeling something dangerously close to excited, wanting badly to
just text him something honest and unguarded — that he was one of the
best people she'd ever argued with, that he made her want to be braver
than she usually let herself be — and talked herself out of it every
single time, because some things felt too big to send at 3am and still
mean the same way in the morning.

The next day at the conference went on like any other — sessions, hallway
small talk, the usual churn of thirty thousand people moving from room to
room.

She still hugged the room full of co-workers, friends, and work-husbands,
easy as ever, zero charge in it, exactly like she always had. And every
single time, across whatever room they were in, he caught her eye first —
not jealous, never that, just checking, the way he'd checked on her that
one night she went quiet mid-argument. She'd give him a look back that
meant *obviously not you, idiot,* and he'd almost smile, and that was the
whole conversation.

## Section 10

The mixer that night was exactly the kind of loud Angel usually tolerated
rather than enjoyed — a ballroom of open bar and forced small talk, music
turned up just enough to make actual conversation an act of will. She'd
done her rounds, hugged the usual faces, laughed at the usual jokes, and
was two drinks into being ready to call it a night when she spotted him
across the room, doing the same math she was.

Callen crossed the floor without making a thing of it. *"This is
unbearable,"* he said, close enough to be heard over the noise, not close
enough to be anything but practical about it.

*"Twenty more minutes and I'm claiming a headache."*

*"Skip the twenty."*

They ended up outside, on a strip of patio behind the venue where the
music thinned to a bassline and the air actually felt like air again.
Neither of them said much at first — just stood there, grateful for the
quiet, close enough that it would've looked like something to anyone who
happened to walk by.

It was Callen who broke the silence, and predictably, not with anything
soft. *"I finished it,"* he said, like it was the most natural follow-up
in the world.

*"Finished what?"*

*"The caching thing. From the forum. Our actual first fight."* A beat.
*"I built it — the pattern, an edge worker, stale-while-revalidate, cache
hit and miss tracked in real time. I wanted to see the numbers instead of
just arguing about them."*

She stared at him. *"You've been building this since when?"*

*"On and off. Long enough that I'm mildly embarrassed about it."*

*"You've been holding a grudge against my duct tape for months."*

*"I don't hold grudges. I hold data."*

*"That is somehow worse."*

*"It's upstairs, if you want to see it. Fair warning — it's genuinely the
nerdiest thing I've built, and that's a competitive field."*

Angel should have said no. She thought about saying no, actually
considered it for about half a second, weighing it against every
potential outcome for the evening.

*"Ya,"* she said instead.

## Section 11

His room was on the ninth floor, the generic kind of upscale-conference
room that could have belonged to anyone — except for the laptop already
open on the desk, three windows deep in code, like he'd been picking at
it between sessions all day.

*"Okay,"* he said, dropping into the desk chair and pulling up a
terminal. *"Try not to be too impressed."*

*"I make no promises."*

He walked Angel through it properly this time — not the shorthand
version from the patio, but the real one: the edge function intercepting
requests, the cache keys, a live dashboard tracking hits and misses in
real time as he refreshed the page over and over like a kid showing off a
science fair project. It shouldn't have been charming. It was,
annoyingly, extremely charming.

*"Look,"* he said, tapping the screen. *"Ninety-four percent hit rate.
Under load. That's the argument. That's the whole thing, right there."*

*"I still think the duct tape works."*

*"I never said you were wrong,"* he said. *"I said you were describing a
floor and calling it a ceiling. I just wanted to know what the ceiling
actually looked like."*

She leaned in to actually look at the numbers, close enough that she
caught the same version of him she'd built out of a hundred late-night
texts — steady, a little smug, entirely sure of himself in exactly the
way that used to annoy strangers on a forum and now did something else to
her completely.

*"You really did this because of me,"* she said. It wasn't a question.

He didn't look away from the screen. *"I did this because you were wrong
and I couldn't let it go. Don't make it romantic."*

*"Too late."*

That got him to look up. Whatever he saw on her face made him go quiet
for a second — not closed-off quiet, just quiet, like he was
recalculating something.

*"Angel—"*

*"I know,"* she said, before he could finish it. *"I know exactly what
you're about to say. Slow. Careful. Don't ruin it."*

*"Something like that."*

*"I'm not asking you to ruin it,"* she said. *"I'm just standing here."*

Neither of them moved for a second. It was Callen, in the end, who closed
the last of the distance — not fast, not dramatic, just certain, the same
way he argued everything he actually believed.

## Section 12

He kissed her the way he argued — completely, like there was nothing else
in the room worth his attention. Slow at first, like he wanted to give her
every chance to change her mind before wholeheartedly proving the
opposite. She didn't take it.

Whatever careful pacing either of them had been managing all night went
out fast, the second his hands actually found her. Months of nothing but
words suddenly had a lot of ground to cover.

She pulled back just far enough to get a full sentence out. *"For the
record,"* she said, *"I'm still right about the duct tape."*

*"Noted,"* he said, already not listening, already kissing her again.
*"Argue with me about it later."*

She did not argue with him about it later.

---

Morning light did something unfair to a hotel room — made everything look
more real than the night had, harsher edges, none of the forgiveness of
low light and someone else's noise a floor below. Angel woke up first,
which felt right somehow, like she needed a minute alone in it before the
rest of the day found them.

Callen woke up a few minutes later, took one look at her already watching
him, and didn't do the thing she half-expected — no scrambling, no
awkward reach for something to say. He just pulled her back down,
unhurried, like it was the most obvious thing in the world.

*"Morning,"* he said, voice wrecked in a way she was going to be thinking
about for the rest of the conference.

*"Hi."*

*"You're doing the thing where you're already overthinking this."*

*"I'm not."*

*"You absolutely are. I can hear it."*

She laughed despite herself, and something in her chest loosened that had
been tight for months without her fully clocking it.

*"Okay,"* she admitted. *"Little bit."*

*"Don't,"* he said. Not a demand. Just quiet, certain, the same tone he
used for things he'd actually thought through. *"Whatever you're about to
talk yourself into — don't. Not yet. Let's just have this be good for a
minute before we make it complicated."*

She let it be good for a minute.

## Section 13

The conference, it turned out, was extremely survivable when you had
something to look forward to on the other side of it.

Angel sat through a full morning of sessions retaining maybe forty
percent of what anyone said, catching herself twice mid-note, pen frozen,
replaying something from four hours earlier instead of whatever the
speaker was actually saying about component architecture. Across the
building, doing his own version of the same thing, Callen texted her once
around eleven — no context, just *"Still thinking about this morning."*
She didn't answer for a full ten minutes, purely on principle, and then
folded completely and told him exactly what she was thinking about too.

By early afternoon neither of them bothered pretending otherwise. She
skipped the last session on her list. He skipped his. Neither of them
apologized for it, and neither of them had to explain where they were
going, because they were both already walking the same direction.

He had the door open before she'd even finished knocking, like he'd been
standing there waiting for the sound.

*"You skipped your talk,"* she said, stepping in.

*"So did you."*

*"That's different. Mine was optional."*

*"Mine was mandatory and I skipped it anyway. Draw your own conclusions."*

She didn't get a chance to draw any conclusions, because he'd already
closed the distance, and this time there was none of the careful
hesitation from the night before — just the simple, dangerous certainty
of two people who already knew exactly what they wanted and had spent an
entire morning being unreasonably patient about it.

It was slower this time, and somehow more reckless for it — less about
proving anything, more about paying attention. He noticed everything; he
always had, that was half of what had gotten her into this in the first
place, and he put that same relentless focus into learning her, unhurried
and thorough in a way that left her genuinely undone by the time either
of them said another full sentence. She stopped being able to hold a
single argument in her head, which for her was saying something.

Afterward, tangled together with the afternoon light doing something
soft and gold through the curtains, she pressed her forehead against his
shoulder and started laughing for no reason she could immediately
explain.

*"What,"* he said, not even a little offended, just curious.

*"Nothing. Just — I came to this conference to give a talk and argue with
strangers about accessibility patterns."*

*"And instead?"*

*"Instead I'm apparently doing this."* She gestured vaguely at the general
situation. *"With you. Repeatedly."*

*"I'd call that a significant improvement to the conference experience."*

*"You would."*

*"I contain multitudes,"* he said, stealing her own line with zero shame
whatsoever, and she laughed again, harder, right into his shoulder, and
didn't bother arguing that one either.

## Section 14

Neither of them said it directly — it just became obvious, somewhere
between round two and the hotel's very reasonable room service menu, that
neither of them was going to make it to the last day of sessions.

*"I have a badge,"* Angel said, staring at the ceiling. *"I paid actual
money for this badge."*

*"You could still go. Nobody's stopping you."*

*"I'm not going to go."*

*"Neither am I."* A pause. *"For the record, I already changed my
flight."*

She turned her head to look at him. *"When?"*

*"About twenty minutes ago. While you were in the shower."*

*"That's insane. That's an insane thing to do."*

*"I know,"* he said, entirely unbothered. *"Change yours too."*

She changed hers too — one more day, one more night, the whole cost of it
a number she deliberately didn't look at too closely, because some
decisions were better made fast and examined never.

They ordered dinner instead of finding it — a room service tray of things
neither of them would normally order, eaten cross-legged on the bed like
teenagers who'd successfully lied their way into an empty house. Callen
made an argument, entirely serious, for eating dessert before the entrée,
which Angel found so absurd she nearly choked laughing — and then found
herself doing exactly that anyway, because apparently that was just how
this worked now, him making a case and her agreeing sooner than she meant
to, every single time.

They put a movie on eventually. Marvel, obviously — two months of
arguing about Phase rankings had earned them a rewatch — and it turned
into exactly the kind of night neither of them would have predicted three
days ago: her narrating spoilers he already knew just to annoy him, him
pausing at random intervals to make a pedantic timeline correction nobody
asked for, both of them slowly giving up the pretense of actually watching
the movie somewhere around the second act.

Later — much later, the movie long abandoned, the room gone quiet except
for the two of them — it happened again, unhurried this time, nothing
left to prove and nowhere else either of them needed to be. It felt less
like something new and more like something they'd already been doing for
a long time, just finally allowed to.

Afterward, in the dark, Callen ran a hand slowly along her spine while
Angel lay there thinking out loud in the specific way she rarely let
herself. *"I have questions about tomorrow,"* she said.

He just smiled — didn't answer right away, didn't push.

*"I don't want to ask them yet,"* she said.

*"Neither do I."*

They fell asleep instead of answering anything, which felt, for one more
night, like exactly the right decision.

## Section 15

That weekend, back in her own house, in her own bed, Angel found herself
doing something she hadn't done in years: checking her phone like it owed
her something.

Nothing came. Not Saturday, not Sunday, not one half-formed thought at
11pm with no context, not even the routine mid-afternoon check-in about
nothing in particular. She told herself she wasn't counting the hours.
She was counting the hours.

She went to the parks anyway, because that was the rule, because
Saturdays were for lightening up — and for the first time in longer than
she could remember, Disneyland almost didn't work. She rode the rides.
She got her churro. She stood in the middle of the happiest place on
earth surrounded by the Disney friends she'd made online over the
years — her own little corner of the internet, the group she'd built and
still ran herself, grown well past a thousand people now. She never went
solo anymore, not really, not with a community this built-in, and most
days that fact alone was enough to fix whatever mood she'd walked in
with.

Not this time. She laughed in all the right places and still spent half
the day doing math on time zones and flight schedules and what, exactly,
silence like this was supposed to mean, which was a new enough feeling
around Callen that it took her most of the day to name it: waiting.

A few months before any of this — before the thread, before the duct
tape, before any of it — her primary client had dropped a link in a Slack
channel with a casual *"hey, you should watch this, I think he built
something we might want to work into our solution."* Angel had glanced at
it, filed it under things to get to eventually, and moved on, because
that was what she did with video links. She didn't learn that way. Never
had. Documentation, tutorials, conference recordings — all of it went in
one ear and straight back out unless her hands were actually on a
keyboard while it happened. She'd embarrassed herself once already trying
to explain that to Callen himself, months later, after he'd sent her some
documentation and asked, entirely reasonably, if she'd read it. She had.
She could not have told him a single thing it said.

So the first video sat unwatched. Then a second link showed up, this one
from a product owner — not a developer, just someone
who'd apparently also stumbled across the same talks and thought they
were worth passing along. Angel let that one sit too, until a third
arrived, and by then it had become enough of a pattern that she got on a
call with the product owner and asked, only half-joking, *"Why does
everyone keep sending me this guy's videos?"*

The product owner laughed. *"Honestly? I think you might think he's
cute."*

Nobody had ever said that to Angel about a developer. Not once, not in
twenty-plus years. She sat with that sentence longer than it deserved,
and then, entirely against her own established rules about video content,
watched the talks that same night.

Her friend was right. Infuriatingly, immediately right. Angel sat there
watching some guy explain caching invalidation strategies with a dry,
unhurried confidence that had nothing to do with the actual technical
content, and thought, distinctly, *oh no.* She didn't catch every
detail — some of it went past her the way video always did — but enough
of it landed that she found herself wanting to actually sit with it
instead of letting it slide by, for once. She got her hands on a keyboard
that same week and built a rough demo of what he'd talked about, just to
see if she'd really understood it or just liked watching him say it. She
emailed the account that hosted the talks and asked, as professionally as
she could manage, whether there were more.

There were. She watched those too.

By the time some anonymous handle showed up in a forum thread a few
months later, arguing that a caching pattern she liked was a bad idea
dressed up as a good one, Angel already knew exactly whose voice she was
reading before she'd finished the first line. She told herself it didn't
matter. So what if he was cute — he wasn't Justin Hartley cute, and
besides, he was clearly smart first, which was the part that actually
counted, which was the part she was allowed to respect out loud. All the
way through those first arguments, through the caching fights and the
naming-convention grudges, she kept the whole thing filed under exactly
one label: *he's a great work resource.* Occasionally, privately, she'd
allow: *fine. A cute work resource.* Nothing more than that. Nothing that
needed saying.

Even the first phone call — the one she still couldn't fully reconstruct,
the one where his actual voice arrived in her ear for the first time, low
and unhurried and exactly the way she remembered it from a screen months
before he ever knew that — she'd told herself was nerves about nothing
more complicated than admiration. She admired the way he thought. That
part was true. It had always been true.

It had just never, if she was being honest with herself for once, been
the whole truth.
